Plug in a keyboard and play

PurrMIDI is a standalone MIDI synthesizer. Connect a USB MIDI keyboard to the board, the STM32 microcontroller generates the sound, and an audio DAC outputs it. No computer needed.

Wiring and first sound

Six sound engines

One plays at a time. Switch with the button on the board or a Program Change from your keyboard. You can listen right now: the web demo plays without the board or a keyboard.

E-Piano

Rhodes-style FM electric piano. The default sound.

Pluck

Nylon-string guitar.

Lead

Analog synth lead.

Organ

Tonewheel organ.

Violin

Physical model of a bowed string.

SoundFont

Any SoundFont2 bank up to 8 MB: each preset in the bank becomes its own instrument.

Build it in an evening

You need only a few easily available parts. Nothing to compile. Soldering is optional too: you can connect everything with DuPont wires.

Board

WeAct STM32H743VIT6 Mini

DAC

PCM5102A module

Keyboard power

USB splitter

Keyboard

Any USB MIDI keyboard. Tested: NUX NTK-61 and M-VAVE SMK-25 MINI.

Audio out

Headphones or speakers; microSD only for your own SoundFont.

  1. Flash the board. Download purrmidi.elf from the releases page and load it with STM32CubeProgrammer.
  2. Wire the DAC to three board pins (PE4, PE5, PE6), power and ground. See the diagram in HARDWARE.md (Russian).
  3. Plug in the keyboard to the board's USB connector. The board does not supply 5 V to the keyboard, so it needs external power (for example, via a Y-splitter).
  4. Power on and play. The display shows a green dot, the instrument name and the notes you press.

About soldering. The common purple PCM5102A module needs the H1L–H4L and SCK jumpers soldered. Finding a module that needs no soldering is on the project roadmap.

How to play

Choose an instrument with the K1 button on the board or with a Program Change message.

Program ChangeInstrument
0E-Piano
1Pluck
2Lead
3Organ
4Violin
5, 6, …SoundFont presets in order
127Next instrument (K1 does the same)

Volume is CC7 and sustain pedal is CC64. Other controllers depend on the instrument; the full list is in ENGINES.md (Russian). The selected instrument and volume are remembered; they survive a power cut only if a 3 V battery is fitted to the VBAT pin.

Your own SoundFont

  1. Name the file SNDFNT.SF2 and put it in the root of a microSD card (FAT32).
  2. The file must be 8,384,512 bytes or smaller. If it is larger, shrink it with the online tool (the file stays in your browser), the SoundFonts/sf2_shrink.py script or the Polyphone program.
  3. Insert the card and power on. Writing to flash takes 30–60 seconds, with progress on the display.
  4. You can remove the card afterwards: it is not used while playing.

Shrink a SoundFont online

A few small ready-made banks are in the SoundFonts folder.

What it does and doesn't do

Does

  • Works as a USB Host with a MIDI keyboard
  • Six engines plus SoundFont banks
  • Shows notes, chords and instrument on the display
  • Remembers instrument and volume

Doesn't (yet)

  • USB MIDI to a computer
  • MIDI DIN input
  • Recording
  • Power the keyboard over USB

FAQ

The keyboard is not detected

The board does not supply 5 V to the connector, so the keyboard needs external power. If the board and keyboard share one power source, use a Y-splitter. The dot in the display header: grey means no keyboard, yellow means enumerating, green means ready.

No sound

On the PCM5102A module the H3L jumper must be set to H (otherwise the DAC stays muted), and the SCK jumper on the front must be bridged. Also check the wiring of pins PE4, PE5 and PE6.

The sound is distorted or very noisy

Most likely the H4L (FMT) jumper is set to H. For I2S format it must be set to L.

How do I change the SoundFont?

Put the new SNDFNT.SF2 on a microSD card, insert it and power on. The bank is rewritten automatically.

Can't find an answer? Describe the problem in Issues: which keyboard, what the display shows and what the UART prints.

Do you need a simpler version?

We are asking you what matters more: ease of building or sound quality.

The PCM5102A DAC gives clean 16-bit sound. But we are thinking about a version without a DAC module: the microcontroller's built-in DAC outputs the sound, and all you need to connect is a 3.5 mm jack and a capacitor. No extra module is needed.

The price of this simplification: the built-in DAC is 12-bit, so quiet notes will sound noisier, and there may be hum from the board's power supply. The output is line-level, for active speakers or an amplifier rather than headphones directly.
